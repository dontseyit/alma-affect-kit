    Simulation¶

    The engine is deterministic and time-step driven: nothing happens between calls, and the same inputs at the same times always produce the same affective trajectories. There is no internal clock, no threads and no randomness, you decide how fast simulated time passes.

    Advancing time¶

    There are three ways to move the simulation forward, all equivalent:

    • update(dt), advance every character by dt seconds; the natural call from a game loop, with your frame or tick delta.
    • step(n), advance in whole update periods (500 ms by default, the paper's rate).
    • run(seconds), simulate a stretch of time in period-sized slices; convenient in tests and offline simulation.

    One update does three things per character, in order: age all active emotions and drop the fully decayed ones; recompute the virtual emotion centre from what remains; move the mood one step (pulled/pushed by the emotion centre, or drifting back to the default mood when no emotions are active). The engine also keeps a running total of simulated time.

    Any step size works, the dynamics are calibrated in seconds, not in ticks, but very large steps make the mood's path coarser: a single ten-minute update moves the mood in one straight line, while the same ten minutes in half-second steps bends smoothly around a moving emotion centre. Frame-rate steps at 60 fps are perfectly fine and cheap.

    Fitting it into a game loop¶

    Treat the affect engine like any other simulation system: give it time every tick, give it events when they happen, read it when you render or decide behaviour.

    • Time: call update with the frame delta each tick, or accumulate the delta and update in fixed 500 ms slices if you prefer a fixed affect rate alongside a variable frame rate.
    • Events: wherever your game already dispatches gameplay events (an insult, a victory, a threat appearing), add a call that lets the affected characters perceive it, either as a named event/act through their rules, or as a direct appraisal.
    • Output: when choosing an animation, a line of dialogue or an idle behaviour, read the character's mood word and dominant emotion at that moment.

    To run affect faster or slower than real time, scale the dt you pass, because the model is calibrated in seconds, scaling time is exactly equivalent to scaling all three time constants together.

    A worked game-loop pattern is on the Examples page.

    What a typical timeline looks like¶

    With publication defaults, a single strong negative appraisal (a very undesirable event, desirability −0.8) plays out like this:

    Time Emotions Mood
    0 s Distress elicited at about 0.8 starts drifting towards the Distress point in PAD space
    10 s decayed to about 0.4 (linear decay) still drifting, more slowly, the emotion centre's intensity fell with it
    20 s fully decayed; the active set is empty begins the slow return to the default mood
    approx 20 min later back at the default mood

    Two things follow from the calibration, and they are the heart of the model's feel:

    • A single emotion barely dents the mood. Twenty seconds of pull at intensity 0.8 moves the mood only about 0.03 PAD units. Moods are medium-term by construction, one insult does not ruin an afternoon.
    • A stream of similar emotions escalates the mood. Repeated appraisals keep the emotion centre alive; once the mood crosses the centre, the push phase drives it deeper into its octant. That is how repeated fear experiences take a character from slightly anxious through moderate to fully anxious, and why a character who has been mistreated for ten minutes stays hostile for a while even after the mistreatment stops.

    Multi-character scenes¶

    Every character appraises the same world input through its own rules, so a single perceived act fans out into different emotions per observer , the attacker, the victim and each bystander all react in character. Perception calls accept an optional list of characters, which is how you scope an input to, say, only the characters in the same room.

    Determinism, saving and restoring¶

    • There is no randomness anywhere. The same calls in the same order with the same time steps reproduce identical states, replays and tests are exact.
    • A character's complete dynamic state is small: its mood point plus its active emotions (type, intensity values, age, cause). The affect-output snapshot is JSON-able and suits save games and telemetry; restoring exactly means recreating the characters and re-adding their active emotions with their ages.
    • Resetting a character drops its emotions and returns its mood to the default mood.

    Constraints and guarantees¶

    • Update requires a non-negative time step; negative values are rejected.
    • The mood always stays inside the PAD cube (−1 to +1 on every axis).
    • Emotion intensities stay in [0, 1] and never fall below the character's personality baseline; an elicitation weaker than the baseline is raised to it and stays active for the decay time.
    • The engine is not thread-safe: drive one engine from one thread, or guard it with your own lock. Separate engines are fully independent.
    • A character's personality, baselines and default mood are fixed when the character is added; changing the shared configuration afterwards affects newly elicited emotions and the ongoing mood dynamics, but does not recompute those per-character constants.

    Performance¶

    Cost per update is proportional to characters × active emotions, with small constants, a handful of float operations per emotion and one square root per mood step. Emotion intensities are memoized per age and personality baselines are precomputed per character. Hundreds of characters at the 500 ms period is trivial; per-frame updates at 60 fps are fine too. Appraisal cost is proportional to the number of matching rules per perceived input.
